Anthropogenic Evolution — The Third Driver of Evolutionary Change

Proposes human cultural, technological, and ideological agency as a third evolutionary driver — alongside natural selection and mutation — tracing its origin to Neanderthal interbreeding and its present in CRISPR, dietary ideology, and the sixth mass extinction.

Evolutionary Biology Epigenetics CRISPR Neanderthal Lamarck Philosophy of Biology
Abstract

This paper proposes Anthropogenic Evolution as a named third driver, which leads to epigenetic changes in humans themselves. 'Third driver' is used here as a naming convention rather than a claim of a mechanistically distinct evolutionary process: Anthropogenic Evolution operates through existing mechanisms, mainly epigenetic inheritance and, in the case of biotechnology, direct genome editing, rather than another category outside them. What is proposed here is the systematic documentation of human cultural and technological choice as an understudied environmental factor operating through those mechanisms, not a new biological pathway. Anthropogenic Evolution is distinguished from natural selection by three properties: Intent — deliberate human decisions, conscious or emergent from cultural systems, that alter which traits persist in a population; Speed — measurable population-level change within decades rather than thousands of generations; and Reversibility — unlike natural selection, it can be reversed, redirected, or halted through changes in human behaviour, culture, or technology.

Academic Engagement

Professor Michael Skinner · Eastlick Distinguished Professor, Washington State University · 17 May 2026

called the central proposal:

“Very insightful.”

— and then identified precisely why it didn't hold as a new evolutionary mechanism: anthropogenic stressors act through the same epigenetic pathways as any other environmental input, whatever their origin. That distinction reshaped this paper's central framing.

Professor Jonathan Andelson · Professor Emeritus of Anthropology, Grinnell College · 9 August 2026

worked through the paper's central claim — that industrialized societies replaced the Darwinian directive of reproductive fitness with a manufactured, structurally infinite directive of status competition — against his own decades of fieldwork on the Amana Colonies, closing the exchange with:

“I enjoyed our exchange.”

His research showed that Amana retained a durable, kinship-based status hierarchy through positions of authority even under communal ownership and cashless egalitarianism, evidence that status competition isn't eliminated by egalitarian structure so much as pushed into subtler form. That distinction, together with his contrasting Hutterite example, gave real ethnographic grounding to a further hypothesis this paper is now testing: that removing one shared achievement directive may not eliminate status competition, but disperse it into multiple parallel status systems.

Professor Alex Mesoudi · Professor of Cultural Evolution, University of Exeter · 16 May 2026

responded to an early hybridization hypothesis connected to this paper:

“That's a very interesting point.”

He then identified a teleological error in it, prompting a shift from a selection-based framing to a byproduct account.

Professor Michael Tomasello · Duke University

identified a further structural gap in that hybridization hypothesis — that it excluded populations native to Africa entirely, a limitation now openly acknowledged and under revision.

Sven Kasser · PhD Researcher in Evolutionary Biology, University of St Andrews · 21 May 2026

was consistently among the most encouraging voices in this correspondence, writing:

“Keep thinking about this kind of stuff.”

He pointed toward existing literature on guided variation and gene-culture coevolution that narrowed the paper's claim toward specific, documented biological outcomes rather than a new evolutionary category. Together, these exchanges moved the paper from claiming a new evolutionary driver to something more precise and more defensible: a documented pattern sitting in the space between Darwinian and Lamarckian inheritance.
