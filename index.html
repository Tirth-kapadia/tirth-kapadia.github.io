ACADEMIC ENGAGEMENT

This work did not develop in isolation. Over the year, it grew through direct correspondence with researchers across neuroscience, philosophy of mind, and evolutionary biology — conversations that shaped, corrected, and in several cases substantially revised the ideas presented here.

Professor Karl Friston
Wellcome Centre for Human Neuroimaging, UCL  ·  22 March 2026

replied to an early draft of the presence-perception hypothesis with the line that set the direction for everything that followed:

“Speed reading your essay suggests that it is extremely well informed.”

He suggested publishing the work on Zenodo — advice that became the starting point for this entire portfolio's existence in citable, public form.

Professor Stephen Fleming
Metacognition Group, UCL  ·  6 May 2026

responded to the metacognition essay with a brief but pointed note:

“Interesting paper.”

What followed was a real challenge to the essay's central claim — that self-modeling represents a qualitative threshold rather than a continuum. Working through Fleming's distinction between individual-level metacognitive accuracy and its evolutionary emergence sharpened the argument considerably, separating what the essay could defensibly claim from what it couldn't.

The Anthropogenic Evolution paper drew a similarly demanding pattern of correction. Professor Michael Skinner
Eastlick Distinguished Professor, Washington State University  ·  17 May 2026

called the central proposal:

“Very insightful.”

— and then identified precisely why it didn't hold as a new evolutionary mechanism: anthropogenic stressors act through the same epigenetic pathways as any other environmental input, whatever their origin. That distinction reshaped the paper's central framing.

Professor Alex Mesoudi
Professor of Cultural Evolution, University of Exeter  ·  16 May 2026

responded to the same framework:

“That's a very interesting point.”

He then identified a teleological error in an early hybridization hypothesis connected to it, prompting a shift from a selection-based framing to a byproduct account.

Professor Michael Tomasello (Duke University) identified a further structural gap in that hybridization hypothesis — that it excluded populations native to Africa entirely, a limitation now openly acknowledged and under revision.

Sven Kasser
PhD Researcher in Evolutionary Biology, University of St Andrews  ·  21 May 2026

was consistently among the most encouraging voices in this correspondence, writing:

“Keep thinking about this kind of stuff.”

He pointed toward existing literature on guided variation and gene-culture coevolution that narrowed the paper's claim toward specific, documented biological outcomes rather than a new evolutionary category. Together, these exchanges moved the paper from claiming a new evolutionary driver to something more precise and more defensible: a documented pattern sitting in the space between Darwinian and Lamarckian inheritance.

Professor Christopher Conway (Department of Psychology, Grinnell College) engaged on the boundary between statistical learning and conscious access — an exchange that led to an independently developed account of three separate factors, sensory competition, social observation, and attentional allocation, that shape what surfaces into conscious awareness.

Professor David Rosenthal
Distinguished Professor, CUNY Graduate Center  ·  10 June 2026

has been the longest and most demanding correspondence, spanning several months on the explanatory limits of a two-mind model of consciousness. His recurring challenge — that simply labeling mental states as conscious or non-conscious does no explanatory work on its own — forced a genuine reworking of the book manuscript's central claims, from a structural description toward an account of the specific causal mechanisms that make a mental state conscious in the first place. Of the exchange, he wrote:

“I am impressed with your thinking.”

What connects all of these exchanges is not agreement — most of them involved being wrong about something specific, and revising accordingly. That pattern, more than any single idea here, is the actual subject of this page.

About

I am a 17-year-old independent researcher from India, having completed higher secondary education in 2026. My work sits at the intersection of neuroscience, evolutionary biology, and the philosophy of mind — specifically the questions that existing scientific frameworks have not yet adequately addressed.

I do not begin with literature reviews. I begin with questions that don't have satisfying answers and build mechanistic frameworks to explain them. Only afterwards do I find the science that confirms, challenges, or extends what intuition had already mapped. That sequence — curiosity first, rigour later — is not a limitation of this work. It is, perhaps, how most original questions begin.

My long-term goal is neuroscience research focused on consciousness — understanding not just the correlates of subjective experience, but what it fundamentally is and why it exists at all. My work on Anthropogenic Evolution, metacognition, and anomalous presence perception all orbit that central question from different angles.
