Metacognition — A New Aspect

Proposes that metacognition is not a rare skill but an architectural default of a brain complex enough to model itself — universal at the baseline, with only access varying, shaped by the conditions Anthropogenic Evolution has made rare and by individual differences between brains.

Metacognition Prefrontal Cortex Cognitive Architecture Threshold Phenomenon Anthropogenic Evolution Philosophy of Mind Discovery Mechanism
Abstract

Metacognition is defined as thinking about thinking, and the field treats it as rare: a skill some people have developed and others have not. This essay questions that assumption. It proposes that metacognition is an architectural default of a brain complex enough to model itself. It emerges when neural complexity crosses a threshold, not because it was selected for as a specific skill, and the appearance of symbolic markers across multiple hominin species within the same geological window is offered as evidence. At the baseline, metacognition is universal. What varies is access to it. Access depends on the conditions under which the default can run (reduced stimulation, lowered cortisol, a prefrontal cortex not exhausted by continuous noise, and the threshold state between waking and sleeping), which Anthropogenic Evolution has made increasingly rare. It also depends on individual differences between brains, which, like any talent, are altered by genetic combination, including tolerance for the isolation those conditions require. The essay argues that research documenting individual differences in metacognition may be measuring access rather than capacity, so the useful intervention is to remove interference first and then refine through practice, not to teach a skill assumed to be missing. Version 3 clarifies the distinction between the universal baseline and unequal access. This is a theoretical essay by an independent researcher and has not been empirically tested.

Academic Engagement

Professor Stephen Fleming · Metacognition Group, UCL · 6 May 2026

responded with a brief but pointed note:

“Interesting paper.”

What followed was a real challenge to this essay's central claim — that self-modeling represents a qualitative threshold rather than a continuum. Working through Fleming's distinction between individual-level metacognitive accuracy and its evolutionary emergence sharpened the argument considerably, separating what the essay could defensibly claim from what it couldn't.
